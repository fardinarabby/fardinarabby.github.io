Industrial attachment · Seven days on site

Payra 1320 MW ultra-supercritical thermal power plant

Roughly a tenth of Bangladesh's generating capacity, sitting on the bank of the Andharmanik river at Kalapara. I spent a week following the fuel path from the jetty to the furnace and the energy path from the furnace to the 400 kV bus.

Why the plant sits where it sits

Two constraints picked the site. The plant burns imported sub-bituminous and bituminous coal shipped from Indonesia, so it needs deep-water access — hence the dedicated coal jetty. And an ultra-supercritical unit rejects a great deal of heat at the condenser, so it needs a large, reliable water source for the closed cooling circuit, which the Andharmanik provides.

How the project was assembled

The plant is owned by Bangladesh-China Power Company (Pvt.) Limited, a joint venture between NWPGCL on the Bangladeshi side and CMC on the Chinese side. The sequence from first memorandum to first concrete took two years, and construction to commercial operation took four more.

Coal handling

Coal is grabbed from the ship at the jetty and moved inland by conveyor through three transfer towers — TT-1, TT-2 and TT-3, with a sampling tower between the second and third — into the storage domes, and from the domes onward to the boiler bunkers. At full load the plant moves roughly 15,197 tonnes a day, about 4.12 million tonnes a year.

Four domes hold 180,000 tonnes each. The dome is not just a warehouse — enclosing the stockpile controls dust, suppresses the fire risk that comes with open coal storage, and lets the plant manage inventory and reclaim rate under cover during monsoon. The stacker-reclaimer arm inside does the feeding.

From the domes coal passes through the crushing house, where it is reduced to a consistent size and homogenised — and where a suspended magnet pulls tramp metal out of the stream before it can reach a mill. Pulverisers then grind it fine enough to burn in suspension, and gravimetric feeders meter it into the furnace.

The steam cycle

The Dongfang boilers run ultra-supercritical: 26.25 MPa at 600/610 °C, above the critical point where the water/steam phase boundary stops existing. That is the whole point — higher steam conditions mean higher cycle efficiency and less coal, and therefore less CO₂ and less ash, for the same megawatt-hour. Quoted efficiency is 94.22% on lower heating value, 87.98% on higher heating value.

It helps to see the numbers to understand the scale. The furnace alone is wider than most buildings I have modelled, and the girder elevation is 87 m above grade.

Steam expands through a single-shaft Shanghai Turbine machine with four cylinders and four exhausts, running at around 270 bar and 600 °C, driving a 660 MW generator at 22 kV. Exhaust steam condenses under vacuum and returns to the feedwater train.

Downstream, the regenerative circuit — low pressure heaters, external steam cooler, deaerator, high pressure heaters — bleeds steam back to preheat feedwater rather than dumping that heat at the condenser. That regeneration is where a large share of the cycle efficiency actually lives, and it was the part of the plant I found most instructive to trace. Turbine heat rate comes out at 7522 kJ/kWh.

Control rooms

Operations are split across four rooms, which tells you something about how the plant is organised. The main control room drives boiler, turbine and generator, manages water and steam flow, regulates SOx and fly ash emission, and owns the grid connection. A separate chemical operation control room handles dosing, water quality monitoring and sample analysis. Coal operation control manages delivery, stockpile, coal quality assessment and the conveyor system. FGD and ESP control handles emissions equipment.

Emissions control

Flue gas desulphurisation strips SO₂ from the exhaust before the stack, using limestone as the reagent. Dry electrostatic precipitators charge the fly ash particles in a strong electric field and collect them on banks of metallic plates, at a quoted 99% collection efficiency. Both are the reason a coal plant of this generation looks different from one built two decades earlier.

Evacuation

Generation at 22 kV is stepped up to 400 kV and leaves through a GIS substation onto a double-circuit 400 kV line into the national grid. Stepping up is what keeps transmission loss down over that distance. The double circuit is redundancy: losing one circuit should not take 1320 MW off the system. Switchgear — breakers, disconnectors, isolators and protective relays — is what makes fault clearing, reconfiguration and maintenance possible without dropping the unit.

The economics, which is the part most site visits skip

A 1320 MW plant is a financial structure as much as a thermal one. Payra was initially agreed at 1.984 billion USD and landed at 2.41 billion. Twenty per cent is own capital; the remaining eighty per cent is a CEXIM Bank loan at 6.66% annual interest over a fifteen-year repayment period. That works out at roughly 1,876.73 USD per installed kilowatt.

The coal consumption rate appears in the plant data as kg/kW·h; at 290.22 the figure is only physically sensible as grams per kilowatt-hour, which is the conventional unit for a standard coal consumption rate.

Because the fuel is imported and the debt is foreign-denominated, the plant carries two exposures that have nothing to do with engineering: the coal price and the exchange rate. As of 30 June 2023 the operating company was showing a gross profit of about 0.27 billion USD against a foreign currency fluctuation loss of 37.27 billion taka — roughly 0.33 billion USD. Seeing that on the same balance sheet was the single most useful thing I took away from the week.

What the week actually taught me

Three things. First, that a power plant is a multidisciplinary object — electrical, chemical, control, automation and safety functions are not adjacent, they are interlocked, and a change in one shows up immediately in another. Second, that safety discipline on a live site is procedural rather than attitudinal; it works because the protocols are rigid, not because people are careful. Third, that the boundary between the technical and financial cases for a plant is much thinner than an undergraduate curriculum suggests.

One recommendation I made in the attachment report. The visit would teach more if it were scheduled after the sixth semester rather than before it. Several of the systems we walked through — the regenerative feedwater train, the protection scheme, the FGD chemistry — only mean something once the corresponding coursework is behind you. As it stood, a good part of the week was spent building vocabulary rather than understanding.
